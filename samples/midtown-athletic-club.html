What a scrolling reader sees (and misses)

  • A catalogue, not a reason — pools, spa, gym, classes, tennis, recovery: every premium club lists the same amenities, so the list gives a reader no reason to choose this one.
  • No number anywhere — your website publishes a striking one — 100+ classes every week, all included — and the ad never uses it.
  • No offer — your site starts every membership with 4 complimentary experiences; a reader of the ad would never know there is an easy way in.
  • No call to action — the Library card shows no CTA button, so even an interested reader has no labelled next step to tap.

What the redesign does differently

  • A number leads — 100+ included classes a week is a concrete fact a reader can compare, instead of a luxury claim every club makes.
  • The amenities support, not carry — pools, spa, tennis, and recovery now back up a headline instead of forming a catalogue.
  • A real way in — the 4 complimentary experiences your site already publishes become the offer, so starting feels low-risk.
  • A button that exists — “Visit the Club” gives the interested reader the labelled next step the current card lacks.

What we changed — and why

Every change below is about what a reader can see in the ad itself: the words, the image, and the button. Nothing here assumes anything about your results — only about how the ad reads.

ElementBeforeAfter — and the reason
Lead messageA luxurious-wellness-studio claim over an amenity listA concrete, published number — 100+ classes every week, all included. Claims are easy to ignore; a specific number is easy to remember and compare.
OfferNo offer, price, or way to startYour own published membership start: 4 complimentary experiences. The easy first step already existed on your website — it was just missing where the audience is.
Next stepNone stated; the copy ends at the amenity list“Visit the club and see it for yourself” — your site’s own invitation, now stated in the ad.
Call to actionNo CTA button shown on the Library card“Visit the Club”. An ad with no button asks the reader to do the work of figuring out what happens next — most will not.
