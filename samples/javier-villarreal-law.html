What a scrolling reader sees (and misses)

  • The headline is a job title — “Accident Lawyer” says what the firm is, not what an injured reader gets or should do next.
  • The copy could belong to any firm — a slogan and a practice label, with no case type named, although the website leads with auto accidents and large-truck (18-wheeler) accidents.
  • No first step — the website starts every case with an initial case evaluation; the ad never mentions it, so an interested reader has nothing concrete to act on.
  • No answer to the reader’s urgency — the firm states it is available 24/7, but the ad gives someone hurt today no reason to contact this firm now rather than the next one in the feed.

What the redesign does differently

  • The reader’s situation leads — the copy opens with the accident and what it costs (lost wages, medical bills, pain), in the website’s own framing, instead of a job title.
  • Case types are named — car and 18-wheeler accidents, the cases the website itself leads with, so the right reader recognises their own situation.
  • A concrete first step — the initial case evaluation your website already offers becomes the ad’s destination.
  • Availability becomes a reason to act — “available 24/7” moves from a website footnote into the ad, where an injured person is deciding whom to contact.

What we changed — and why

Every change below is about what a reader can see in the ad itself: the words, the image, and the button. Nothing here assumes anything about your results — only about how the ad reads.

ElementBeforeAfter — and the reason
HeadlineA job title only: “Accident Lawyer”A first-step headline — start with a case evaluation. An injured reader is looking for what to do next, not for a label; the headline now answers that question.
